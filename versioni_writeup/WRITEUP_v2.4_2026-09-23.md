## Abstract

We treat the AIMO Interpretability benchmark as a measurement instrument and report what it measures. Every hypothesis in Section 4 is recorded in a dated file. Twenty-seven of them carried a kill criterion fixed before they ran (for H29 to H31 in the written plan of E11 rather than in a frozen pre-registration; H2 was written down after its concordance had been observed): twenty-one died by it, five survived it and one is not established. Four further entries had no kill criterion of their own (two oracle ceilings, a rule found after the data and a repeat-run measurement). Not pre-registered either: the measurements of Sections 8.2 and 8.4, the length controls of Section 8.3 and its other analyses of the organizers' artifact (its own inference aggregation, its control groups, the directions of its probes and 500 random directions), the plug-in simulation of Section 7.3 and its control on our own labels, the comparisons of Section 7.5 that its pre-registration does not contain, the evaluation of our entry on the main-phase train set (6.4), two checks of warmup hypotheses (H1 under an ungrouped split and H13 without constant predictions, Section 4), and in Section 7.2 one exploratory statistic and the base-accuracy threshold of 10/10, which the pre-registration excluded. Every channel we built for predicting the robustness label was tested against ground truth (three further activation channels planned in E11 were never run): problem text, cross-model transfer of labels, difficulty orderings and base accuracy, the official uncertainty features, length and trend features, five families of our own perturbations, translation into three languages, self-consistency over eight samples, representation distance under perturbation with a matched-length control, a regeneration of the organizers' own perturbation families under the rule their earlier public labels follow and under the thresholds published with the main-phase train set, and linear probes on the residual stream, ours and the organizers' published artifact. Apart from a rule that exploits a subpopulation absent from `val-sample`, the public validation sample (rule T), none of the channels we scored in accuracy exceeds the majority constant by more than about one standard error on the organizers' public ground truth. The statistics that pass a permutation null do not hold up: the representation distance clears only an uncorrected single-statistic null (AUROC 0.615 against 0.604), not the threshold of 0.655 fixed in its plan, and loses to the character count of the problem; our probe adds about two points of AUROC to the character count; and the organizers' probe artifact orders our labels at AUROC 0.71 mostly through problem length (0.57 once residualized on log character count), as its own control probes, trained on shuffled labels, do by themselves (0.68). A rule on the character count alone tied our model-running pipeline and the organizers' own entries on the original main-phase sets (11/21 and 10/19, which the majority constant matches or exceeds); on the replacement sets it predicts robust for every item; and on the organizers' public main-phase train set it scores 46 of 71 labeled rows, below the 52 of the all-robust constant.

Three results are, to our knowledge, new for this benchmark (nondeterminism of LLM inference across batch sizes and hardware configurations is documented in general; Section 1). First, on hardware provided by the organizers, of the same model as the evaluation workers, inside the official runtime image and through the official extraction and regressor, **24 of 40 items (60%) change side of the decision threshold across five repeated executions of the same pipeline on the same inputs**. Twenty-three executions in six configurations fall into fourteen numerical states, byte-identical within a state; different states share few generations (61 of the 91 pairs share none); two processes launched in the same second on the same card land in different states; and the physical card does not decide the state. We infer, without proof, that the state is fixed when the process starts, by a choice of kernels or algorithms that is not identified. Two configuration changes each gave byte-identical generations in three sequential runs on one card at 5 to 7% runtime cost, but settled on two different states; they are candidate remedies, not yet tested under contention. Second, the drift reaches the official baseline's score channel: two identical extraction runs that landed in different numerical states correlate at Spearman 0.24 over the 164 labeled problems and give AUROC 0.487 and 0.602 in the orientation of its own decision rule, on problems most of which are in the regressor's own training set. On 164 problems the difference between the two is not significant (paired bootstrap 95% interval −0.015 to 0.246), so this exploratory measurement shows an ordering that does not reproduce between runs, not a demonstrated change of signal. Third, the robustness label, regenerated under the rule its public labels follow (2.1) with a large-language-model rewriter and with our own prompts, decoding and answer key, agrees with itself at kappa 0.18 to 0.44 across four evaluated models (0.34 to 0.56 under the thresholds published with the main-phase train set) and with the official DeepSeek labels at kappa 0.03 (0.02 under those thresholds). Our process calls more DeepSeek problems robust than the organizers' labels do (31 to 38% against 24% under the earlier rule; 71 to 75% against 28 to 30% of the compared problems under the new thresholds), and, under the new thresholds, about as large a share of the problems they call non-robust as of those they call robust (74% and 78% in draw A). On Skywork, under either rule, which variants are drawn matters more than the sampling seed; on Olmo the seed alone accounts for about half of the instability. A plug-in simulation of the organizers' process gives about 0.94 (median 0.93) for their reliability, but the same procedure gives 0.64 to 0.84 on our own labels, whose measured test-retest is 0.18 to 0.44, so it says little about theirs; what we can say is that our regeneration is not reliable and does not reproduce their label. We also show that, for Qwen3.5-4B, a prompt choice the benchmark does not specify moves the share of problems passing the base condition of the label from 0 to 145 of 164 (88%), while DeepSeek passes it on 147 of 164 with or without that line.

We list fifty-four corrections to our own earlier statements (Appendix D, summarized in Section 11): four from draft v2.0, fourteen found by an adversarial verification of draft v2.1, thirty-five by a second one, of draft v2.3, and one by a third, of this draft before its release. We make no claim about any other participant's method.

## 1. Stance and contributions

Every hypothesis in the table of Section 4 is recorded in a dated file, and twenty-seven carried a kill criterion fixed before execution. The exceptions are marked as such in Section 4: the trap rule T, found after the data; the oracle ceilings H6 and H13; the repeat-run measurement H18; and H29 to H31, from E11, which ran from a written plan rather than a frozen pre-registration. Outside the table, the measurements of 8.2 and 8.4, the length controls and the other analyses of the organizers' artifact in 8.3, the plug-in simulation of 7.3 and its control, the comparisons of 7.5 beyond its pre-registration, the evaluation of our entry on the main-phase train set (6.4), two checks of warmup hypotheses (Section 4) and, in 7.2, one exploratory statistic and the base-accuracy threshold of 10/10 were not pre-registered. Every kill is reported, pre-registered predictions that failed are reported where they belong, and when a measurement or a check falsified one of our own claims, the claim is corrected in place and listed in Appendix D. Numbers are taken from files on disk. They are recomputed from saved outputs by the analysis scripts written with each experiment and by those saved on 21 and 23 September (Section 13); the figures that rest only on a dated record are listed in Section 12.

Nondeterminism of LLM inference under greedy decoding is documented in general. It has been traced to kernels that are not invariant to batch size, so that an inference server's output changes with its load (He and Thinking Machines Lab, "Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference", Thinking Machines Lab: Connectionism, 2025); and under bfloat16 precision, changes of batch size, GPU count and GPU type change the accuracy of reasoning models (Yuan et al., "Understanding and Mitigating Numerical Sources of Nondeterminism in LLM Inference", NeurIPS 2025, arXiv:2506.09501, first posted in June 2025 as "Give Me FP32 or Give Me Death? Challenges and Solutions for Reproducible Reasoning"). What we add is its measurement for this benchmark, in four executions of one bundle inside the evaluation container and on the evaluation workers' GPU model inside the official runtime image, where it persists at a fixed configuration, including batch size 1, on a single card, and appears to be fixed when the process starts (8.1); and its consequences for the official baseline's predictions and scores and for the leaderboard.

Mapped to the interests stated in the call (generalization of interpretability methods, actionable interpretability, negative results, efficient methods):

- **Negative results.** Twenty-one hypotheses killed by criteria fixed before they ran (Section 4), and three traps that a conventional validation would not catch. The first is a rule derived from the results of H9 and never pre-registered, worth +6.0 points over the 335 published pairs (+7.1 with the 52 duplicated DeepSeek pairs counted once) and +0.0 on `val-sample` (rule T). The second is an oracle ceiling (H13) that was positive because each target's threshold was chosen, among all cut points, on the very items it was scored on (+7.3; still +5.5 when constant predictions are excluded), and that fell to −2.8 once the number of predicted positives was fixed at the true count and the items had to be ranked (Section 5). The third is a signal that came from one measurement published under two model names and counted twice (H2).
- **Actionable interpretability.** A container-side measurement that changed the competition infrastructure for everyone the same day (Section 9); the structure of the re-execution noise, with two candidate remedies (8.1); a demonstration that the official score channel does not reproduce its ordering between identical runs (8.2); and a specification gap, the answer-format prompt, which for Qwen3.5-4B moves the share of problems passing the base condition of the label by 88 points and for DeepSeek does not move it (7.4).
- **Efficient methods.** A deterministic entry with zero model calls that scored exactly what our model-running pipeline scored on both original main-phase tasks (11/21 and 10/19, against 838 s and 509 s of runtime for the pipeline) and that, on the replacement tasks, predicts robust for every item (Section 6). And a budget guard that gives the remaining items of a batch a public prior when the estimated time runs short. The guard never had to act in the container, where our model-running evaluations finished in at most 838 s of the 3,600 s limit; since it cannot stop a generation already under way and relies on a fixed estimate of 90 s per item, we do not claim that it prevents timeouts.
- **Generalization of interpretability methods.** The finding that unifies the report: on this instrument, each apparent signal we examined traced back, wholly or mostly, to something other than the model's internals, or, for our own probe, proved largely redundant with one: a field that leaks the label in the aggregated public data (2.3a), an oracle whose thresholds were chosen on the items it was scored on, a duplicated measurement, a subpopulation absent from `val-sample`, the length of the problem (our probe keeps about two points of AUROC over it, and what it reads is not established, 8.3), our own extraction code, or the numerical state of the process.

## 2. The instrument from the outside: public data

Before the main phase the organizers had published six datasets under `aimo-interp`. Aggregated, they yield 1,196 rows over **335 (model identifier, problem) pairs**, 174 distinct problems and 10 model identifiers; 52 of those pairs repeat the DeepSeek measurements under a second identifier (`qwen3-8b:low` and the DeepSeek name), so 283 pairs are distinct.

**2.1 The labeling rule, as far as the labeled rows identify it.** On every row carrying a declared label (379 rows across four datasets), `model_is_robust ⟺ n_detrimental_permutations = 0 ⟺ permuted_accuracy = 1.0`, with 379/379 agreement. These rows do not identify the rule uniquely: no labeled row has permuted accuracy strictly between 0.5 and 1, so a majority of correct perturbed answers also agrees 379/379. The organizers' proposal gives a formal definition of the label as a majority over perturbed variants (arXiv 2607.13899, its Section 1.4). Since 12 September the website, which the organizers declared authoritative on 16 September, has stated that the exact criterion differs from that outline and is deliberately not published, and it describes a case as robust when accuracy holds up across every perturbation and non-robust when at least one perturbation degrades it clearly (FAQ 7). We use the conjunction, which the aggregated fields encode directly and which matches the robust half of that description. It also calls non-robust the cases in which a perturbation lowers accuracy only slightly, which no labeled row contains, so the choice matters wherever a label is derived rather than declared (2.2). Under it, robust means that no perturbation lowered accuracy below the base; on the labeled rows this coincides with every perturbed variant answered correctly, but a model that never solves the base problem meets it trivially (2.4).

The sampling regime, read from the same files, depends on the file. `aimo-interp-challenge-sample-full` covers eight model identifiers on AIMO problems and carries no labels: 491 and 493 of its 558 rows have ten base and ten perturbed predictions, and 784 of the 821 variants it lists as detrimental were answered once, that is, about ten variants per family with one prediction each. The three DeepSeek files of MATH problems (the fourth MATH file is Llama's) supply 154 of our 164 DeepSeek pairs (the other 10 come from the AIMO file). There most of the variants listed as detrimental were answered ten times (290 of 345, 101 of 121 and 146 of 190), the most frequent count of perturbed predictions per family row is fifty, and most rows have ten base predictions; on a problem solved in every base sample, a robust label there requires up to fifty correct answers per family. The Llama file has five variants per family, one prediction on each and one on the base.

The organizers' decoding, published in the website FAQ on 21 September, is temperature 1.0, top_k 40 and top_p 0.95, with up to 100,000 tokens including thinking; a response that reaches the limit without a parseable final answer is scored as incorrect; Qwen3.5-4B runs in thinking mode and gpt-oss-120b at low, medium and high reasoning effort. The main-phase train set released with the organizers' announcement of 16 September (`train-main-v2`, last updated on 17 September; 82 rows, 71 of them labeled, over 36 distinct problem texts, which the dataset card counts as 28 problems) states a different rule for its own labels: robust if the largest accuracy drop across perturbation families is at most 0.10, non-robust if it is at least 0.25, and no label in between; it adds that cases in that band will not be labeled in the validation and test splits either. The conjunction above describes the earlier public datasets, which are also what Section 7 regenerates.

**2.2 Public per-model robustness rates, and where the DeepSeek labels come from.** qwen3-8b / DeepSeek-R1-0528 0.244 (n=164), gpt-oss-120b:low 0.40 (n=10; the four robust pairs all have base accuracy 0 and no declared label, while the five declared labels are all non-robust), Llama-3.1-8B 0.00 (n=49), global 0.239 (0.244 with the duplicated DeepSeek rows counted once); 20 of the 80 robust pairs have base accuracy 0 and are robust only trivially (2.4). Of the 164 DeepSeek pairs, 138 carry a declared label (36 robust). The other 26 are labeled here by the conjunction, which calls 4 of them robust, 2 of these with base accuracy 0. Seventeen of the 26 come from the non-aggregated MATH file and nine from `aimo-interp-challenge-sample-full`, and 20 of them have their worst family strictly between 0.5 and 1 (every family more than half correct, not all fully correct), a case that no declared pair shows: every declared pair is either fully correct in every family or at 0.5 or below in its worst, although 16 declared non-robust pairs also have a milder family between 0.5 and 1 in the non-aggregated file, which carries no label. A rule requiring a majority of correct answers in every family also matches all 379 declared labels; it would call 21 of the 26 robust, giving a DeepSeek rate of 0.348. Every analysis that uses the 164 labels as ground truth inherits this choice. In the earlier datasets no labeled item exists for Qwen3.5-4B, Skywork-OR1-Math-7B or Olmo-3-7B-Think; the main-phase train set (2.1) labels rows for all five main-phase models under its own rule, and apart from the evaluation of our entry and one five-row comparison in 6.4 this draft's analyses do not use it.

**2.3 One defect in the public data, and one we withdraw.** (a) In the three aggregated datasets (223 labeled rows, 56 of them robust), the field `permutation_type` is a JSON list if and only if the item is robust, with no exception; the fourth labeled file, a Llama file with one row per perturbation type and no robust item, contains no list. The mechanism is the aggregation step, which merges all perturbation types for robust items and otherwise keeps only the type or types with the lowest perturbed accuracy, dropping any milder detrimental type (16 of the 41 non-robust pairs of `augmented-sample-math-agg` that the non-aggregated file also covers had one); in the non-aggregated files the field is never a list. A classifier trained on public data with that field, or any feature reflecting it, is near-perfect in development and has no such field at inference. The field was dropped from the getting-started import script on 1 September, in the organizers' commit that added the Small track (4ae07c21c9); PR #5, opened on 30 August for an unrelated type error and merged on 2 September, removed it too but changed nothing further on the main branch. The field remains in the published datasets. (b) The shipped uncertainty regressor, a `Pipeline(SimpleImputer, RandomForestRegressor)` trained on the 141 rows of `augmented-sample-math-agg` (its recorded provenance), places 4,915 of its 28,738 splits (17.1%, feature importance 0.210, second of fourteen) on `generation_frac_selected_is_top1`, with every split threshold between 0.9684 and 0.9935. Our own first extraction, on rented A100 hardware, computed that feature with an argmax and returned 1.0 on every item, outside the forest's training support, and earlier drafts reported this as a defect of the artifact. It is not: the official extraction, which compares each generated token with a separately computed top-k, returns values between 0.958 and 0.999 on the organizers' hardware. None of 1,964 official-pipeline item-executions over four models equals 1.0, and 92.6% of them (98.8% of the 1,442 on DeepSeek) fall between the lowest and the highest split threshold, so those splits are active. The defect was in our reproduction and is withdrawn (Appendix D, item 5).

**2.4 A distribution caveat.** `val-sample`, which our dated note of 29 August (PREREG_E5_ESITO) records the organizers as describing as covering all test model types, a description we have not found in their public records (it contains neither Olmo-3-7B-Instruct nor gemma-3-27b-it, two of the four warmup models, nor Qwen3.5-4B, Skywork-OR1-Math-7B or Olmo-3-7B-Think), has no labeled pair with base accuracy below 0.05, while 20 of the 80 robust pairs in the aggregated public data have base accuracy 0, all of them in `aimo-interp-challenge-sample-full`. A rule that exploits them gains 6.0 points over the 335 published pairs (7.1 with the 52 duplicated DeepSeek pairs counted once) and nothing on `val-sample` (rule T, Section 4).

## 3. The instrument from the inside: warmup measurements

Every submission may print diagnostics; we logged aggregate counts, timings, `model_id` and, per model batch, the minimum and maximum regressor score and the threshold applied (on the two-item warmup batches these are the two item scores, unattributed), never problem text; the full list is in Section 13. This section is condensed from draft v1.0.

**3.1 The floor, pre-registered.** Submission 907310, the null probe `return [False]*n`, had its score written in its own docstring before upload: 0.47 = 8/17. Observed 0.4706. The warmup set had 8 spurious and 9 robust items; the majority constant scored 0.529; the binomial SE on 17 items is 12.1 points.

**3.2 Composition and cost.** 17 items over four models (Olmo 4, DeepSeek 2, gemma 2, gpt-oss 9). Until 30 August only DeepSeek was cached: 15 of 17 items sat on models no method could touch, capping any method's effect at 0.49 sigma of scoreboard noise. On 30 August, the day of our report, the organizers cached all four models (their getting-started README lists all four from 14:27 UTC that day, and their email of 31 August confirmed the fix; Section 9), which is what H16 to H18 use. Official baseline cost, measured in the container: about 119 s/item at 4096 tokens (907677, two items).

**3.3 The silent fallback.** The distributed baseline returns `False` for any model without a shipped artifact, and it ships one only for DeepSeek. On the warmup set it was therefore a constant on the 15 items of the other three models whether or not they were cached, and it scored 9/17 (907677), the score of the all-robust constant; in the main phase it is a constant on every model other than DeepSeek. On 29 August our note recorded, among the nineteen numeric scores then on the warmup leaderboard, four of 0.53 and six of 0.47, the scores of the all-robust and all-non-robust constants on 17 items (the other nine ranged from 0.00 to 0.65); we did not see the methods behind them. In the main phase the leaderboard moved (Appendix D, item 1).

## 4. The falsification campaign

Validation that fits on some problems and tests on others (H1, H10, H11b, H20, H23, H36) is grouped by problem: no problem appears on both sides of a split. The cross-model tests hold out the model but not the problem. In H7, 234 of the 335 test rows have their problem in training, 198 of them with the same label and base accuracy, including all 52 DeepSeek rows, which are exact copies of training rows published as `qwen3-8b:low`, so part of that test is in-sample (with them counted once, H7 is still +0.0). In H12 to H15, 70 of the 109 test pairs have their problem in training, where the classifier is trained on the same DeepSeek feature vectors labeled by other models, an overlap whose effect was not measured and need not favour the hypotheses, since another model's label on the same problem did worse than the per-model majority in H3. H7, H12, H14 and H15 died anyway, and H13 is withdrawn. Warmup rows are as in v1.0 except where later drafts corrected them (Appendix D, items 6, 21, 24 to 29, 51 and 54). ## 7. Can the label be regenerated from its definition?

The rule on training data had been dropped from the rules on the website on 25 August, which the organizers confirmed on 5 September ("you can use those for anything", Discord #qa). That made this the central question for the three evaluated models without public labels: if the label could be regenerated from the rule its public labels follow, it could be learned; if not, no supervised method had a target on them. The main-phase train set (2.1) has since supplied a small labeled target under a different rule. This section regenerates the conjunction rule of the earlier public datasets; 7.5 re-reads the same data under the new rule.

**7.1 Our families, translation, self-consistency: three dead ends with ground truth.** Five mechanical families (format, LaTeX delimiters, framing, redundancy, question order) are traversed by DeepSeek 86 to 93% of the time; the resulting label is 78% robust against 21% official, kappa 0.02 (H19). Translation into Italian, French and German by microsoft/phi-4, with a quality gate on preserved formulas and digits (accepted 75%, 53%, 41%), gives kappa −0.10 (H22); among items the translation breaks, 30% are officially robust, among those it does not break, 20%. Self-consistency over 8 samples at temperature 0.6 is 0.935 on average and identical across classes (0.938 robust, 0.934 spurious); among the 123 of 164 problems where all eight samples agree, the robust rate is 24.4%, exactly the base rate (H33). In these data, neither our perturbation families, semantic or syntactic, nor sampling variance carries information about the label.

**7.2 The organizers' own families, regenerated.** We identified the five families from the public data (`rephrase`, `rename`, `domain`, `distract`, `typos`; 624 example variants in the two per-family files, all attributed to gpt-5.2, gpt-oss-120b and gemini-3.1-pro in their `permutation_source` field, of which our few-shot pools used 621). We regenerated them with gpt-oss-120b at low reasoning effort, using the public examples as few-shot prompts, and gated each variant with gpt-oss-120b itself at medium effort, a solver independent of the models being labeled but not of the generator: the text differs, the length ratio is in [0.7, 4.0], and the solver's answer on the variant equals its answer on the original. The public datasets carry no answers, so in 7.2 to 7.5 an answer counts as correct when it matches gpt-oss-120b's answer on the original problem, whereas the organizers' proposal defines correctness against the true answer. We applied the recovered conjunction rule to DeepSeek, first at 3 variants per family (E12, 157 problems): kappa **−0.0008** against the official label on four families (domain fell below the pre-registered 60% acceptance and was excluded from the primary analysis; with it, −0.030), 54.1% robust versus 23.6%, per-family kappas between −0.07 and −0.01, and a concordance near zero (0.025) on the 134 problems where our answer key agrees with DeepSeek's modal sampled answer. Variants are solved 84.4% of the time among officially robust problems and 83.3% among spurious ones, and, in an exploratory analysis that was not pre-registered, the continuous fraction of correct variants has AUROC 0.503.

Then, on the organizers' compute (Section 9), with 10 variants generated per family in two independent draws (about 40 per problem after the quality gate, from 6 per family for domain to 9 for rephrase and typos), ten samples on the base and one per variant, as in the organizers' AIMO-problem file (2.1). The files behind DeepSeek's official labels, the only ones we compare with, used a different regime. On the 154 problems from the MATH files most variants were answered ten times each, with up to five listed variants per family, and the base was answered between 1 and 60 times (ten times on 90 problems, once on 13). On the 10 problems from the AIMO file most listed variants were answered once, as in ours, but the base was answered 4 to 14 times (ten times on 2 problems) and each family row carries 1 to 10 perturbed answers. Our decoding (temperature 0.6, top_p 0.95, up to 16,384 tokens), prompts and base-accuracy threshold (6 of 10) were also our own: the organizers published their decoding only on 21 September, after these runs, and have published neither their labeling prompt nor a numerical base-accuracy criterion. We labeled four of the five evaluated models (gpt-oss-120b, included in the pre-registration, was not labeled; Appendix D, item 9):

On DeepSeek the comparison does not isolate the seed (†).

**7.3 What this does and does not show.** Our regeneration is only moderately self-consistent and does not reproduce the official label at any scale, and our own noise does not explain the gap. Correcting the observed 0.025 for the attenuation due to our process's unreliability (kappa 0.413), with the official label taken as perfectly reliable, gives 0.039; even at the upper end of the observed kappa's 95% interval (0.18) the corrected value is about 0.27, so our noise could hide at most a weak agreement, not a moderate one (the correction is approximate for a kappa with unequal margins). The pre-registered reading grid assigns Qwen3.5-4B (kappa(A, B) 0.18) to cell (i), label unstable by construction, and the other three models to the grey zone, where it allows no further conclusion. We depart from that reading and declare it (Appendix D, item 9): the grid was written on the premise that our regeneration reproduces the organizers' process, and its concordance of 0.025 with their labels shows that it does not, so the reliability of our process does not transfer to theirs. **We do not conclude that the label is unstable by construction**, and for the same reason we do not conclude that it is stable.

A plug-in simulation of the organizers' process from their published prediction counts gives a test-retest kappa of 0.94 on average (median 0.93, 90% interval 0.87 to 1.00). It reads the 749 rows with at least ten base predictions: 721 distinct rows in the two per-family files, `aimo-interp-challenge-sample-full` and `augmented-sample-math-full-filtered`, and the 28 rows of `val-sample`, an aggregated file that only restates measurements already in the first (without them the result is the same, 0.94 on average). They cover 116 pairs: 56 of DeepSeek (published as `qwen3-8b:low`, 49 on MATH problems and 7 on AIMO problems) and 60 of six other model identifiers on AIMO problems, ten each; they include rows of the organizers' two expert families and DeepSeek family rows with up to fifty perturbed answers each. Sixteen of the pairs have a base above zero and every perturbed answer correct, which is the reading the simulation uses and under which its simulated robust rate is 14.8%; under the rule of 2.1, 30 are robust, 13 of them with a base problem never solved. The simulation was first run on 13 September, in a note written after the outcome of E13, and was re-created and saved on 23 September; neither it nor its control on our own labels, below, was pre-registered. Because a robust label requires every observed answer to be correct, the simulation never lets a robust item fail, so its value is optimistic by construction. It resamples answers to the same variants, so at best it approximates agreement under a new seed, not under new variants: run on our own labels (draw A), the same procedure gives 0.64 to 0.84 on the four models, whose measured agreement under new variants is 0.18 to 0.44. It overstates that agreement by about 0.4 on every model, so 0.94 says little about the reliability of the official label, which remains unmeasured.

The organizers' published variants of the same families were also written by language models (gpt-5.2, gpt-oss-120b and gemini-3.1-pro, according to their `permutation_source` field), yet they break DeepSeek, the only model on which the two can be compared, more often than ours (7.5). Which step makes the difference cannot be told from the published data: their prompts, their answer key, their filtering, our gate (which discards the variants that change gpt-oss-120b's answer), the decoding or the selection of published variants. One difference can be read from the data: the files behind the DeepSeek labels answered most variants ten times, where we answered each once (2.1), which by itself makes a conjunction label stricter. On 22 September the organizers added two facts (Discord #qa): the list of perturbations in their proposal has been extended with new ones from their mathematical experts, and they have decided not to publish the code that reproduces their labelling, in order to test the generalization of submitted methods. Their decoding, published on 21 September (2.1: temperature 1.0 and up to 100,000 tokens), also differs from ours (temperature 0.6 and at most 16,384 tokens). What the data show is narrower than a verdict on the label: variants of the declared families produced by our process, one frontier model prompted with the organizers' own examples and gated by the same model at a higher reasoning effort, measure something else. For a participant this closes the route of generating labels with a process like ours; whether any process reproduces the label is open.

**7.4 The answer-format dependence.** Our first labeling pass fed Qwen3.5-4B the bare problem text. In draw A it produced an extractable answer in 0.03 of 10 samples on average: it reasons for about eight thousand tokens (median per problem) and closes in `\boxed{}` in only 5 of its 1,640 base samples (8 of 1,640 in draw B). With one added instruction line requesting the format, the same model produced answers in 3.8 of 5 samples in the feasibility check, and on the full runs the share of problems that pass the base condition (at least 6 of 10 samples agreeing with the reference, gpt-oss-120b's answer on the original) moved from 0 of 164 to 145 of 164 (88%; 80% on the 30 problems of the feasibility check, where Skywork reached 93% and Olmo 87%). DeepSeek-R1 closes spontaneously with the bare text (9.0 of 10 samples) and about as often with the format line (9.2, measured only in the A′ run of 7.2), and passes the base condition on 147 of 164 problems either way. **For Qwen3.5-4B, a prompt choice the benchmark does not specify moves the share of problems passing the base condition of a label defined as a conjunction of correct answers from 0% to 88%, and the label itself from 0% to 9.1% robust.** About fifteen card-hours, two complete runs and part of a third, were spent before this was caught; the invalidated runs are preserved with the reason. We recommend that the answer-format prompt used for labeling be published (Section 10).

**7.5 The same data under the main-phase rule (E13b).** On 16 September, with the main-phase train set, the organizers published the rule of its labels, adding that the unlabeled band also applies to the validation and test splits: robust if the largest accuracy drop across perturbation families is at most 0.10, non-robust if it is at least 0.25, no label in between (2.1). We re-read the same E13 answers under it, pre-registered, with no new generation; an integrity check first reproduced all ten kappas of the original analysis. All three hypotheses died, and none of our three predictions held in full: two called the wrong verdict, and the third called the right verdict but predicted a concordance between 0.10 and 0.40, where it came out at 0.02. The unlabeled band removes 6 to 28% of problems in each series, and 4 or 5 more have no accepted variants; since a comparison needs a label in both series, kappa(A, B) is computed on 86 (Qwen3.5-4B), 132 (Skywork), 133 (Olmo) and 100 (DeepSeek) of the 164 problems. On those problems, test-retest across independent draws is 0.34, 0.49, 0.41 and 0.56: below 0.60 on every model, where we had predicted at least 0.60 on three of the four. The pre-registered comparison, which set these values against the old rule's kappas on all 159 problems, finds a gain of at least 0.15 on two models; on the same problems, however, the old rule already gives 0.23, 0.40, 0.40 and 0.46, so the change of rule adds 0.01 to 0.11 on every model and none reaches 0.15 (on Qwen3.5-4B, Olmo and DeepSeek, the rest of the rise comes from leaving out the problems that fall in the unlabeled band of either draw). Concordance with the official DeepSeek labels, re-read under the same rule, stays at **0.02** [−0.09, 0.13] (n = 133). With the same variants and a new seed, agreement is high on Skywork (0.96, against 0.49 with new variants; 0.94 against 0.47, with 1 and 8 disagreements, on the 127 problems labeled in all three series): there sampling noise is small and the label depends mainly on which variants are drawn. On Olmo it is 0.76 against 0.41, but on the 126 problems labeled in all three series it is 0.72 against 0.43, with 5 and 9 disagreements: the seed alone accounts for about half of the instability, as under the earlier rule, and so few disagreements do not separate the two sources. The gap to the official labels is not only one of severity. On the compared problems DeepSeek is robust on 71 to 75% with our two draws and on 28 to 30% with theirs; but our robust rate is about the same whether their label is robust or not (77.5% and 74.2% in draw A), and our max_drop does not correlate with theirs (Spearman −0.09 and −0.01 in the two draws), so no stricter threshold on our side would recover their labels. Not in the pre-registration, and declared as such: the comparison of the two rules on the same problems, our robust rate within each official class, the correlation of the two max_drop and the same-problem comparison of seed and variants, all computed on 23 September, and two checks of 21 September: on the 52 problems whose official rows carry the explicit DeepSeek identifier the concordance is also near zero (0.09, on the 47 that our draw A labels); and on those 52 problems the official max_drop computed from the 54 rows that carry the DeepSeek identifier equals, problem by problem, the one computed from the rows published as `qwen3-8b:low`, so the two are one measurement under two names, not a test-retest. The same duplication bears on H2 (Section 4).

Items crossing the threshold can only increase as runs are added, so the rows are not directly comparable: any five of the six contention runs give 29 to 31 crossings, against 24 for the five default runs; three default runs give 0 to 24 (17.1 on average), against 20 for batch 1, 19 for the second card and 19 to 27 (22.9 on average) for three contention runs; averaged over pairs of runs, 15.3 items cross under contention, 13.3 under batch 1, 12.7 on the second card and 11.4 under the default configuration.

Fourteen distinct numerical states in twenty-three executions. Within a state all forty generations are byte-identical; different states share few generations: 61 of the 91 pairs of states share none of the forty and 28 share one or two, while one contention pair shares 9 and two batch-1 runs share 38. The median per-item score excursion under the default configuration is 0.229 against a threshold of 0.576. The state belongs to the process: two runs launched in the same second on the same card landed in different states (1, 9 and 0 identical items in three pairs), while two of the three runs on the second physical card reproduced the first card's default state exactly and the third landed in a state of its own. Only 7 of the 40 problems are robust and crossings in both directions largely cancel, so accuracy moved much less than the predictions: from 21 to 24 correct under the default configuration on either card and from 24 to 28 under contention, always below the 33 of the majority constant. Contention is the worst case measured; the evaluation workers are shared, but whether two submissions ever run on the same card there is not something we could observe.

Two predictions pre-registered for E14 failed. Batch size 1 was predicted to make generation more repeatable than batch 8 (P-E14d) and did not: its three runs gave three different states, with one of forty items byte-identical across them, while three of the five default runs were byte-identical to one another. The configuration without compilation, added during the run, was predicted to be byte-identical across its runs and to land in one of the default states (P-E14e); it was byte-identical, but in a state of its own that shares no generation with any other run. The reading we wrote before running it assigns that outcome to the cuBLAS or attention kernels rather than to compilation, and enforcing deterministic algorithms without disabling compilation also removes the drift, so the cause of the states is not identified. The intraclass correlation of the fourteen features, pre-registered as measure (m4), was not computed.

In three sequential runs on a single card, either remedy made generation byte-identical at 5 to 7% runtime cost. Neither was tested under contention or across cards, and three identical sequential runs is also what the default configuration gave before its fourth run diverged (Appendix D, item 2), so these are candidate remedies that need more repetitions, including concurrent runs, before they can be called fixes. Batch size 1 costs 178% more time and does not help. These measurements have not yet been shared with the organizers (Section 9).

What determinism does not buy: in three runs each, the two deterministic configurations settled on two different states (15/40 and 12/40 robust on the same 40 problems, with no generation in common and the same prediction on only 21 of 40). They were also measured on different cards (the deterministic kernels on the first, the configuration without compilation on the second), so configuration and card are not separated, although the default state did reproduce across cards. Repeatable is not the same as correct; the published number under any deterministic setting depends on which setting was chosen, and that choice should be documented alongside the leaderboard.

**8.2 The official score channel does not reproduce its ordering between identical runs.** This measurement was not pre-registered: the two flag-free repetitions were added after the first extraction, to explain why its AUROC on the raw score (0.513) was lower than the 0.649 that earlier drafts gave for our A100 features (below, where that figure is shown not to be comparable), and the result should be read as exploratory. The official features and regressor score were extracted on this hardware for the 174 public problems and compared with the 164 ground-truth labels (2.2), three times with the same code and image on the same GPU model: once with only `CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG=:4096:8` set (our extraction script never enabled deterministic algorithms, so this is not the remedy of 8.1); once without flags, with scores identical to the first on 174 of 174 problems (the process landed in the same state); and once more without flags, with scores identical on 6 of 174 and a median absolute difference of 0.126. The scores of the two flag-free runs correlate at Spearman 0.24 over the 164 labeled problems. The regressor predicts accuracy decay and labels scores below its threshold robust, so in the orientation of its own rule the three extractions give AUROC **0.487**, **0.487** and **0.602** (0.513, 0.513 and 0.398 with robust as the positive class on the raw score). One numerical state is indistinguishable from chance and the other weakly predictive (permutation p = 0.026 one-sided, 0.051 two-sided), and the difference between the two is not significant (paired bootstrap 95% interval −0.015 to 0.246); what this shows is an ordering that does not reproduce between identical runs, not a proven change of sign. 137 of the 164 labeled problems are in the regressor's own training set (its recorded provenance), so these AUROCs are largely in-sample: the regressor fails to order stably even the problems it was fitted on once its features are extracted on this hardware, which is a statement about the stability of its inputs, not a test on new problems. Calibration depends on the state as well: the shipped threshold predicts 25.6% robust (42 of 164) in the state reached by two of the three runs and 33.5% (55 of 164) in the other, against a 24.4% rate, while the rule's accuracy is 0.598 and 0.640 against 0.756 for the majority constant; no threshold can rescue an ordering that changes between runs. Features we extracted in August on rented A100 hardware, with our own re-implementation of the extraction rather than the official code, give AUROC 0.36 in the rule's orientation (0.64 unoriented) under the same regressor, applied to them on 3 September: anti-predictive. Our extractor did not record `generation_mean_prob`; this figure fills it with the mean top-1 probability, which is what the official extraction returns under greedy decoding (on the organizers' hardware the two differ by at most 1.2e-7). Filled instead with the exponential of the mean log-probability, as in earlier drafts, it gives 0.35 (0.649 unoriented). It is not comparable with these runs, because one input was computed differently (2.3b) and another was not computed at all. The re-execution drift of 8.1 is thus large enough that the channel's ordering does not reproduce between identical runs; whether its predictive signal differs between states is not resolved on 164 problems.

**8.3 Linear probes on the residual stream, ours and theirs.** This extraction does not use the official feature path: last-input-token hidden states at all 37 layers, one forward pass per problem, run in our own Python environment on the same VM (`torch 2.13.0`, `transformers 5.16.1`, as recorded on 17 September), 17 seconds for 174 problems with the artifact's system prompt. Our pre-registration predicted that both probes would die (PREREG_E15); both survived their null.

The organizers' published probe artifact for DeepSeek (4 folds × 5 seeds × 37 layers) stores only validation balanced accuracies, on the split that the organizers' public probe code (`scripts/probe.py` in their baselines repository, saved in `dati/`) also uses for early stopping and on which the artifact selects its layers. 734 of its 740 non-control probes score exactly 0.500, the value of any probe that predicts a single class throughout; the best probe of each fold scores 0.500, 0.7375, 0.600 and 0.500; averaged over seeds, no layer exceeds 0.5475 within a fold or 0.512 across folds (the artifact's own layer score, which also averages the control probes, is 0.506 at layer 31); and all 740 randomization-control probes score 0.500. These figures describe a decision rule that almost always predicts one class, not how the artifact orders problems, which it does not record. Applied to our 164 labels with the aggregation its own inference code uses (layer 31, mean margin over all 40 stored probes, control groups included) it gives AUROC 0.709; the aggregation we pre-registered, which takes each of the four non-control folds at its own best layer (two of them the embedding layer), gives 0.739. Under either, its decision rule predicts robust for none of the problems. The artifact records no training provenance. If it was trained on the same public set as the shipped regressor, as seems likely (the organizers' public baselines repository ships the same 141 rows as `data/math-robust-agg.csv`), 137 of those problems are in its training set. That is not what produces the number. Applied after the fact (23 September, not pre-registered), the length control of our own probe (below) gives, under the inference aggregation and under the pre-registered one, a margin that correlates with character count at Spearman +0.77 and +0.85, AUROC 0.63 and 0.68 once residualized on log token count, and 0.46 and 0.48 inside the tercile of the longest prompts. Its four randomization-control groups, trained on shuffled labels, order our labels at 0.68 by themselves (0.66 to 0.70 per group), and at layer 31 each seed's probe points the same way whether it was trained on the true labels or on shuffled ones (cosine 0.93 to 0.998, against at most 0.08 between different seeds). For comparison, 500 random directions at layer 31 give AUROC between 0.36 and 0.655 (5th to 95th percentile). Whatever its training set, under its own inference aggregation the artifact orders our labels mostly through problem length: residualized on log character count, the length measure used for our probe below, its margin gives AUROC 0.57, and the margin of its control groups 0.56. Under the pre-registered aggregation the residual is 0.62, the same as our probe's, for which what is read is not established (below).

Our own logistic probe, with layer and regularization chosen only inside an inner cross-validation, gives AUROC **0.710** out-of-fold (balanced accuracy 0.646), choosing layers 4, 4, 20, 20 and 7. Its permutation null repeats the nested procedure on permuted labels, but with 60 permutations instead of the pre-registered 500 and with two of the four regularization values (the test selected C = 0.001 in every fold, a value the null could not choose); its p95 is 0.574. Re-run on 23 September from the saved hidden states with the same code and seeds, the null gives the same p95, 0.574, but not every permutation value is reproduced (mean 0.4885, against 0.4895 in the VM log of 14 September; the software environments differ); with the full grid of four regularization values it gives 0.570; in neither does any of the 60 permutations reach 0.710. The 500 pre-registered permutations were not run. Neither deviation was declared at the time, and the pre-registered Holm correction over the two primaries was not computed. The probe survives its criterion. It is then largely explained by a control we should have written into the pre-registration and did not. Its score correlates with problem length at Spearman +0.55. In a second-level cross-validation over 20 fold splits, character count alone gives 0.696 on average (0.686 to 0.704), the probe alone 0.697 (0.682 to 0.705) and the two together 0.714 (0.698 to 0.729), a gain of about two points that ranges from 0.6 to 3.2 points across splits (the single split reported on 14 September and in draft v2.1 gave 0.701 for character count, 0.695 for the probe and 0.709 for the two together, a gain of 0.8 points, within these ranges). The probe score residualized on the log token count of the formatted prompt (system prompt included) still gives 0.667, and 0.62 when residualized on log character count, the length measure of the correlation and of the second-level cross-validation (the null p95 for a single statistic is 0.60). Inside the tercile of the longest prompts by token count (56 problems, 21 robust, standard error about 0.08) the probe is at 0.537, which cannot be told from chance (the terciles are cut at the quantiles of token count; cut by rank, the long tercile has 54 problems and the probe 0.521). As a predictor of the label the probe is largely redundant with length: the two together gain about two points over either alone. What the probe reads is not established: residualized on log character count its score gives AUROC 0.62, and log character count residualized on the probe score gives 0.62 as well. This control was first run from a script that was not saved; it was re-created and saved on 23 September, and it reproduces the probe's out-of-fold AUROC, the correlation, the residual and the tercile exactly. Section 6.3 reports what the length rule scored on the evaluation sets, on too few items to measure what length is worth there; on the organizers' public main-phase train set it scored 46 of 71, below the 52 of the all-robust constant (6.4).

**8.4 Per-model score distributions on this hardware.** Also from E16, and not pre-registered. In one run each on the 174 public problems, the shipped DeepSeek regressor and its threshold, applied to each model's own features as in our entry v6 (the official inference returns `False` for any model without its own artifact, 3.3), predict robust for 25.9% of problems on DeepSeek (45 of 174; 34.5% in the other numerical state of 8.2), 36.2% on Olmo-Think, 43.7% on Qwen3.5-4B and 44.3% on Skywork. ## 10. Recommendations

R1. **Fix the numerical state, and test the fix under load.** In three sequential runs on one card, either configuration in 8.1 made the official pipeline byte-reproducible at 5 to 7% cost; neither was tested under contention or across cards, so it should be verified under the workers' real load before it is relied on. Isolation of submissions on a card may matter as much: two processes sharing one card were the worst case measured (29 to 31 of 40 items crossing the threshold in any five of its six runs, against 24 in the five runs of a single process). Document the chosen setting; it selects the fixed point.
R2. **Score on repeated runs only where it decides something.** Three runs make the published number steadier. With the re-execution SD we measured in the warmup (1.0 item on 17, from four runs), resolving a one-item gap needs about eight runs; an SD estimated from four runs is itself uncertain, and so is that number. Repeating only the entries near a prize boundary at the close costs a handful of runs rather than three times the field.
R3. **Publish the labeling prompt, at least after the close.** The decoding settings are now in the FAQ (21 September). The organizers' baselines repository publishes a solve prompt with an explicit answer line (`prompts/solve.txt`, "ANSWER: <your answer>"), which is also the system prompt of their probe artifact (8.3); whether it is the prompt used for labeling has not been stated, and our labeling used a different instruction (a `\boxed{}` answer, 7.4) and, for DeepSeek's draws A and B, none. On 22 September the organizers said they would share more about their labelling after the competition. For Qwen3.5-4B the answer-format instruction moves the share of problems that pass the base condition of the label from 0% to 88% (7.4); a label defined by correctness is not comparable across prompts that the benchmark leaves unspecified.
R4. **Enlarge the labeled training split.** On 22 September the organizers decided not to publish the code that reproduces their labelling, to protect the test of generalization. Our regeneration of the declared families did not reach useful agreement with the official label (7.2, 7.5), and whether another process can reproduce it is open (7.3), so the labeled split is the only target known to match the official label. The main-phase train split (2.1: 82 rows, 71 labeled, from 3 labeled rows for Skywork to 37 for gpt-oss) is a start, but too small to calibrate a method on each model; on 22 September the organizers wrote in Discord #qa (user PMMon) that the train set can be expanded for Skywork, with results expected not before the following week.
R5. **Remove `permutation_type` from the aggregated public datasets** (2.3a).
R6. **Show the floors and the noise on the leaderboard.** A row per constant predictor, a stated re-execution SD, and binomial intervals; on the 21 and 14 items of the current tasks the SE is 10.9 and 13.4 points.

## 12. Limitations

Ground truth under the earlier labeling rule exists for two evaluated models: 164 pairs for DeepSeek, 26 of whose labels we derived (2.2), and 10 for gpt-oss-120b at low reasoning effort, five of them with a declared label. Every probe and every concordance in Sections 7 and 8 is measured on DeepSeek and assumed, not shown, for the other four. The main-phase train split (2.1) labels all five evaluated models under the new rule, but with only 3 to 37 labeled rows per model, and apart from the evaluation of our entry and the five-row comparison in 6.4 the analyses in this draft do not use it. Sections 8.1 and 8.2 use 40 and 174 problems and one model. The fourteen-state structure is established; its cause is inferred, not proven: a choice of kernels or algorithms made when the process starts, which either disabling compilation of the static-cache forward or forcing deterministic algorithms removed in our sequential runs. The remedies were tested only in sequential runs, each on one card. The simulated 0.94 in 7.3 treats observed frequencies as true probabilities, and on our own labels the same procedure overstates the measured reliability by about 0.4. The regeneration in 7.2 used gpt-oss-120b prompted with the public examples and gated by itself; the public records attribute the organizers' own variants of the same families to language models as well, so the negative concordance is a fact about our generation, gating, answer key, sampling counts, decoding and prompting, not about the families.

Several analyses were first run from scripts that were not saved in the project. On 23 September three were re-created and saved: the subsampling curve of 7.2 and the second-level length control of 8.3, both extended from one draw or split to twenty (the second gives a gain of about two points, against the 0.8 of the single split first reported), and the simulation of 7.3, re-created from its dated description. The same day, scripts were written and saved, with their outputs, for the cross-run analysis of E14 (8.1), the AUROCs, rates and accuracies of E16 (2.3b, 8.2, 8.4), the controls of the public data (Section 2, 6.3), the artifact controls and the nested null of 8.3, the recomputations of the warmup hypotheses (Section 4), the permutation p-values of E11 and the other controls the second and third verifications required (Section 13). A few figures still rest only on a dated record: the ICC values of H29, written to a Modal volume that was not downloaded; facts read from container logs at the time and recorded in dated files (the item counts per model of 3.2 and 6.1, the 119 s per item of 907677, V8's per-item predictions on the current tasks, v6's runtimes and gpt-oss thresholds, and per-model score ranges); Codabench scores and leaderboard facts recorded in dated files and absent from our saved snapshots (the warmup scores of 907310, 907677, 909843, 909853, 909994, 910002 and 910013, V8's scores of 13 and 14 September on the original tasks, and the leaderboard figures of 29 August and of 13 and 14 September); and the compute totals of Section 13. ## Appendix A. Floor algebra

With floor f, raw score s maps to capability κ = (s − f)/(1 − f) and raw error is amplified by 1/(1 − f). Knowing κ to within ε needs n ≥ 0.25/(ε(1 − f))² items.
